# Scope a Client Project Through Quotation

Turn the available project material into understandable, traceable scope, then justified technical choices and costs. Respect the agreed allocation of product and delivery responsibilities. An agent recommendation remains a proposal until adopted.

## When to Use

Use for scoping from a brief, requirements document, email, conversation, meeting notes, call summary, transcript, or a combination of these sources. Prepare clarification questions, draft or revise client-facing requirements, compare suitable solutions, or prepare an estimate. Start at the requested stage using the evidence and decisions already available. Deliver that stage without requiring the entire process.

## When Not to Use

Simple wording changes without scope implications, isolated technical research, implementation, and deployment belong to their own workflows. This skill does not authorize sending material to the client or making external changes beyond the user's request.

## A1 — Establish What Is Actually Requested

Project material can mix requirements, suggestions, and decisions. Confusing them turns ideas into commitments. Read the available sources and maintain a compact requirements state with each item's origin and status: confirmed, proposed, or requiring clarification. Track formal approval separately when relevant. Use the existing working document rather than creating a parallel record.

When the delivery team also owns product definition, develop the workflows, product presentation, and application material from the available business evidence. Distinguish what the team prepares, what the client must supply or approve, and what requires an external provider's decision. Include agreed registration and setup assistance in the delivery scope rather than treating it as work the client must perform alone.

A supplied brief can establish requested scope without a new interview. In a transcript, distinguish speakers, exploratory suggestions, and decisions. In notes or a summary, preserve the stated attribution and uncertainty. When an omitted detail or ambiguous attribution materially affects scope, cost, or approval, check the original source if available. Otherwise identify the uncertainty. An omission from a summary does not establish that a requirement was rejected.

A previous assistant's suggestion, estimate, or description of a vendor feature is not a client requirement. Carry explicit client or delivery-team decisions forward. Identify real contradictions and their consequences. Revisit an established decision only when new information challenges it.

Describe users, workflows, and relevant money flows before choosing a product category or architecture. Business subscriptions do not determine the whole experience. Public browsing, company operations, and field work may have different needs. A requested capability does not, by itself, determine the interface or application format.

## A2 — Ask Only Questions That Change the Next Decision

A long questionnaire shifts the analysis burden onto the client. When clarification is needed, propose at most six plain-language questions in the requested language, each with a short business example where useful. Extract existing answers from all supplied material first. If it already supports the requested deliverable, proceed without a questionnaire.

Choose questions by the decision they enable: who uses the product, what they do, who pays whom, or which constraint changes scope or cost. Ask for observable outcomes rather than frameworks, APIs, server rendering, or native applications. Derive technical options from those needs.

Before asking, determine what would change depending on the answer. If nothing changes for the requested stage, defer it. An unknown budget can remain unknown while preparing scope.

Preserve coherent requested capabilities together. Propose scope reduction only when an identified constraint justifies it, explain the trade-off, and keep it marked as proposed. Clarification must not become an obligation to choose a single workflow.

## A3 — Draft and Revise Requirements by Meaning

Adapt each client-facing document to the action expected from its reader. Requirements should explain what each user will be able to do, in business language with useful examples. Preparation documents should state what the client must provide, review, decide, or understand to proceed. Keep implementation details and provider-specific research in the technical analysis unless they change a client action or commitment. Preserve coverage through common requirements and clearly marked conditional items. Identify proposed boundaries without presenting them as accepted.

Classify each item before placing it in a section:

| Ref. | Role | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Capability | Describe the functionality to deliver within scope. |
| C2 | Configurable setting | State who can configure it and when. Its final value may not be necessary to estimate the capability. |
| C3 | Open business decision | Explain what remains undecided and whether it actually blocks the current stage. |
| C4 | Assumption or proposed boundary | Make it visible and preserve its status until validated. |

For example, creating subscription plans, setting prices, and selecting included features is an administration capability. Final values can be set later when that configuration is included in scope. They do not automatically become prerequisites for quotation. Any promise of “no additional development” must be limited to the supported settings.

When asked to rephrase or simplify, preserve useful information. Move it when its classification is wrong. If an item serves several roles, distribute its content appropriately rather than moving or deleting the whole block. Renumber lists and update affected references.

For an authorized persistent edit, read the current version and use the tool's protection against concurrent changes. After writing, reread affected and neighboring sections to verify preserved content, placement, order, and references. Tool success does not establish document consistency.

## A4 — Compare Solutions Before Recommending a Stack

The goal is to cover the requested workflows at a reasonable total cost. Start with reusable solutions and the delivery team's established preferences. Examine relevant families: customizable products, embeddable engines, targeted components, and custom development. A few familiar vendors are insufficient when another family could change the decision.

Map credible candidates to requested workflows, distinguishing provided, configurable, custom, and unknown coverage. Verify decisive claims against the sources that own them, using `factual-research` when available. Evaluate integration, existing interfaces, customization, maintenance, licensing, and cost. Account for synchronizing accounts, data, and business rules when estimating reuse savings.

When a single solution does not cover the requirements, evaluate credible combinations of complementary providers. Verify the complete workflow, including transfers between systems, approvals, reconciliation, refunds where relevant, responsibilities, and integration costs. Individual capabilities do not establish that the combined arrangement is supported.

Compare the preferred stack and alternatives against the same requirements-derived criteria. Where relevant, assess public discovery, search visibility, catalog browsing, and purchasing. For field work, assess phone usage, connectivity, and required device capabilities. Justify each additional application or service through an established need and include its coordination cost. Available framework features do not prove a measured benefit for this project.

When payments are in scope, distinguish subscriptions, purchase collection, fund allocation and beneficiary payouts, and any direct payment. Separate the operating company's country, buyer countries, and beneficiary countries. Verify integration effort, test access, platform eligibility, and beneficiary approval. A published API or created account does not establish authorization for all these flows.

Stop exploring once relevant families have been examined and decisive criteria support a selection or conditional recommendation. Present useful candidates and consequential exclusion reasons. A question such as “would this other technology be better?” calls for comparison. Change the recommendation only by identifying the new evidence or requirement that justifies it.

## A5 — Estimate Costs From Scope and Coverage

A budget becomes useful when its assumptions are visible. Estimate from workflows, main screens, integrations, and work remaining after reuse. Identify required people and distinguish effort from calendar time, especially when vendor activation depends on a third party.

Use the user's selected configuration as the budget baseline. Present cheaper configurations as alternatives with explicit trade-offs rather than silently replacing selected resources. State workload assumptions and free-tier limits, including background activity that can affect usage-based charges.

Separate initial development, licenses, recurring operations, variable fees, and human maintenance. Distinguish promotional pricing, renewal pricing, monthly equivalents, and upfront payments. Give units, usage assumptions, and sources for externally priced amounts. Keep unknown costs explicit. For a preliminary budget, provide a conditional range and its main sources of variation.

When a price or timeline has already been communicated, preserve it as the commercial baseline. Label a proposed allocation of a fixed total separately from an independently estimated cost breakdown. Check that deliverables include agreed assistance and setup work. Flag any mismatch with estimated effort or unresolved dependencies rather than silently changing commitments.

Do not turn documentary research into practical validation or an exploratory range into a firm quotation. Identify unknowns that could materially change the price. Recalculate affected items when reuse, payment, interface, or scope decisions change.

## A6 — Reconcile the Work After Each Correction

A local correction can leave the same error elsewhere. Identify the incorrect assumption, classification rule, or missing criterion. Update dependent requirements, comparisons, and estimates where affected.

Before delivery, check that the result covers confirmed requirements, preserves proposals as proposals, includes only questions needed at this stage, and remains consistent with prior decisions. Report what was reread, documented, or tested and what remains unverified. Do not claim installation, publication, or client approval that did not occur.
